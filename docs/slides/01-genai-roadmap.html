Orientation

Where this starts

You are not behind. You are unsorted.

Open any GenAI thread and the terms arrive all at once, flat, with no indication of which ones are for you: RAG · RLHF · quantization · vector DBs · PEFT · LLMOps · prompt engineering.

Where do I start?

Every roadmap you find starts somewhere different, and all of them look plausible.

Do I need to know this one?

Half of these terms are for people who train models. You may never train a model.

How do I keep up?

A new model ships every week. Chasing releases is not a learning strategy.

This session answers all three with one diagram and one habit — and the habit is the part that keeps working long after the slides are over.

PART ONE

What generative AI is

Where it came from, what it broke, and why it earned the attention it got.

What Gen AI Is

Definition

A machine that produces work, not predictions

Generative AI is AI that creates new content — text, images, music, even code — by learning patterns from existing data, mimicking human creativity.

The operative word is new. Not retrieved, not looked up, not picked from a list. Written, for the first time, for you.
Put that definition next to what AI could do five years ago and the size of the shift becomes obvious. That comparison is the next three slides.
What Gen AI Is

Historical context

AI is sixty years old. Most of it didn’t work.

Techniques came and went, each one certain it was the answer:

  • Symbolic AI and expert systems — the big idea of the 1980s. Encode human rules by hand, and keep encoding.
  • Fuzzy logic — reasoning in degrees of truth rather than true/false.
  • Evolutionary algorithms — breed and mutate candidate solutions.
  • NLP and Computer Vision — two separate fields back then, each with its own toolbox and its own conferences.
One approach outlasted all of them: machine learning. Stop writing the rules. Feed a statistical model a large amount of data and let it extract the patterns itself. Still relevant, still hiring.
What Gen AI Is

The old job description

Classical ML answered questions. It never made anything.

Problem type What you asked it What came back
Regression What will this company’s stock close at tomorrow? A number
Classification Is this photo a cat or a dog? A category
Ranking Given this product, which products are most similar? An ordered list
Look at the shape of every output: a number, a label, an ordering. Nothing on this table required creativity — and the standard line was that AI never would. Within two years, that sentence stopped being true.
What Gen AI Is

The nested-circles model

GenAI is not beside AI. It is inside it, four levels down.

What Gen AI Is

Already happened, not coming soon

Four industries that have already changed

Customer support

Serving customers at scale used to mean a call centre full of agents. Now a chatbot is the first layer and only unresolved queries escalate. Where ten people were needed, two or three may now do.

Content creation

Blogs, websites, video, social. The honest test: you can no longer reliably tell whether an article was written by a person. The output quality is that good.

Education

Planning a curriculum, getting unstuck at 1am, generating practice questions. Effectively a personal tutor that is always available — and schools are being forced to rethink how they teach.

Software development

This one was supposed to be safe — creative, intellectual work. But models write production-ready code, and you can change an application by writing a prompt. Where five programmers were needed, two or three may do.

Real problems, daily utility, economic impact, new job titles. GenAI passes every test for a successful technology — which is why it is worth a durable mental model rather than chasing headlines.

PART TWO

The mental model

One term at the centre, two directions out of it, and a habit you can run on every new word you meet.

The Mental Model

Step 1 — put one term at the centre

Foundation models

  1. Very large scale

    A very large number of parameters. The architecture itself is huge.

  2. Trained on roughly everything

    Close enough to “all of the internet” that the distinction stops mattering.

  3. Enormously expensive

    Many GPUs and crores of rupees to train once. This is why so few organisations build them, and why almost everyone else uses them.

  4. Generalised, not specialised

    The key property — and the one everything else in this deck hangs on.

That fourth point is the clean break with classical ML. The next slide is what it actually means.

The property that matters

One model, many jobs

The Mental Model

A note on the word

Why it says “foundation model,” not “LLM”

LLM — Large Language Model

Text in, text out. The backbone of GenAI as most people meet it, and the thing you will spend most of your time building on.

LMM — Large Multimodal Model

Images, audio and video as well as text. Same idea, same scale, a wider set of inputs and outputs.

“Foundation model” is the term that covers both. But if it helps, mentally substitute “LLM” every time you see it — nothing in this roadmap breaks.

PART THREE

Two curricula

What each side actually asks you to learn — and which one you should open first.

Two Curricula

Curriculum A · builder’s perspective

Build a foundation model and ship it to the world

More technical, and closest to what ML and DL practitioners already do. It has real prerequisites — this is the one side you cannot start in the middle of.

  • Machine learning fundamentals
  • Deep learning fundamentals
  • A DL framework — TensorFlow or PyTorch. PyTorch preferred.
If those three are missing, the seven modules that follow will not land. That is useful information, not a discouragement — it tells you which side to open first.
Two Curricula

Curriculum A · the pipeline, first half

From architecture to a model that fits on hardware

  1. Transformer architecture

    Every current foundation model is built on it. Encoder side, decoder side, embeddings, self-attention, layer normalisation, language modelling.

  2. Types of transformers

    Encoder-only, decoder-only, encoder–decoder — and the actual architectures of BERT, GPT and the other well-known models.

  3. Pre-training

    Training objectives, tokenization strategies, training strategies (local vs cloud, single machine vs distributed), the problems that only appear at this scale, and how to evaluate whether pre-training went well.

  4. Optimization

    Foundation models are too large for normal hardware. Optimisation during training, model compression (quantization, knowledge distillation), and inference-time work to cut prediction latency.

Two Curricula

Curriculum A · the pipeline, second half

From a trained model to a deployed one

5 · Fine-tuning

Adapt the generalised model so it performs better on a specific task or task-type: task-specific tuning, instruction tuning, continual pre-training in a domain, RLHF and PEFT.

6 · Evaluation

Thorough evaluation across many metrics before anything ships. This is also what powers the LLM leaderboards — “model X beat model Y” is this step, published.

7 · Deployment

Last, and not optional. An undeployed model is of no use to anyone.

Seven modules, in that order. This is the pipeline a data scientist at an organisation that actually builds foundation models runs through — which is a much smaller set of organisations than the internet implies.
Two Curricula

Curriculum B · user’s perspective · step 1

Building basic LLM apps

Less technical than the builder side, and — honestly — more fun. You get to build things. It starts with one decision: which model, reached how.

Closed-source models

Someone else hosts them. You reach them over an API and pay per token. Nothing to run, nothing to size.

Open-source models

You run them — locally or on your own server — with tools like Hugging Face or Ollama. More control, more responsibility.

Then a framework to hold the application together, which is where LangChain comes in — and where the rest of this playlist lives.
Two Curricula

Curriculum B · step 2

Three ways to improve what comes back

Prompt engineering

The art and science of writing the input you send a foundation model. Sounds trivial, is not — there is already a large body of work to study here.

RAG

A model is trained on its own data and knows nothing about yours. RAG shows it your private documents and does question answering over them. A whole field on its own.

Fine-tuning

The same word as on the builder side, at a shallower depth. Here you adapt a ready-made model; there you reach into the deeper layers.

These three are where most of the real engineering work in GenAI applications currently sits. Reach for them in that order — prompting is cheapest, fine-tuning is costliest.
Two Curricula

Curriculum B · steps 3 to 5

Agents, operations, and everything else

  1. AI agents

    An LLM app is usually a chatbot. An agent is a chatbot that can also act. Ask for the best places to visit in India, it says Goa — then you tell it to book the hotel, and it does. Mechanically: you give the model tools it can call. Chatbot + tools = AI agent.

  2. LLMOps

    The umbrella for taking an LLM application to production: deployment, evaluation, monitoring, continuous improvement. A growing set of libraries exists to help.

  3. Miscellaneous

    Multimodal foundation models — audio in, video out, and every other combination — plus diffusion-based models like Stable Diffusion.

Agents are the fastest-moving item on this list, and the one most worth your attention after the basics. It is also the one most often confused with the model itself.
Two Curricula

The question everyone asks

Should you learn both sides?

Yes — but the weighting depends entirely on the role you are aiming at.

If your goal is Focus on Because
Research scientist Builder side Plus ML engineering and MLOps. This is data-science work.
App developer using LLMs User side Anyone with a software development background can do 80–85% of this work.
AI Engineer Both, in parallel The profile that is actually scarce.
The valuable profile is someone who works on the user side but understands how foundation models are built. A software developer can build an AI application today. Someone who knows both sides can explain why it behaves the way it does — and command a better salary for it.
Two Curricula

What to carry out of here

Five things, and the last one is the point

  • Foundation models are the centre of gravity of the entire GenAI landscape. Start every explanation there.
  • Every GenAI term is either “building a foundation model” or “using one.” Classify first, study second.
  • The user side is more accessible; the builder side is more differentiating. Aim for both if you want to be an AI engineer.
  • GenAI passes every test for a successful technology — real problems, daily utility, economic impact, new jobs, broad access.
  • Do not chase every new release. Fit each new thing into the model; most of it is a new instance of a slot you already understand.
Two Curricula

Back to the start

A sentence each, as promised

Where do I start?

On the user side, unless you already have ML and DL fundamentals. It needs no prerequisites you do not have, and you can ship something this week.

Do I need to know this one?

Classify it first. Builder-side terms are not your problem yet — knowing that a word is someone else’s job is itself progress.

How do I keep up?

You stop trying to. You keep up with the slots, not the releases — a new model is a new instance of something you already understand.

If those three now feel obvious, that was the whole job — and notice that none of the answers required you to know a single model name.
